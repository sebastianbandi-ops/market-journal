When demand outruns the forecast

By Sebastian Bandi

If someone handed me a forecast for a new data center, my first question would be when the customer expects to draw power. A year on a slide leaves a lot open.

What makes a forecast move?

PJM’s account of its annual review of large electricity users explains how it evaluates proposed projects before including their demand in its long-term forecast. A proposed connection carries uncertainty about both timing and size. Read the PJM Inside Lines article.

That distinction matters to someone financing new generation. A plant can be ready on schedule and still face a very different market if the customer it was built to serve arrives two summers later.

What one afternoon can tell us

My dashboard gives me a way to practice asking about timing on a smaller scale. At hour ending 14 on August 13, the AEP zone used 20,916 megawatts against a forecast of 20,389.

That gap of 527 megawatts tells me where to start looking. Before assigning a cause, I would check the weather assumptions, compare neighboring zones and see whether the gap persisted into the evening.

One afternoon cannot settle a question about a decade of growth. It does suggest a habit worth carrying into a deal review. I would ask to see cash flows under three different customer start dates, with the same plant in each case.
