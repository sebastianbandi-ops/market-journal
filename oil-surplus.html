Cheaper crude can still mean an expensive delivery

By Sebastian Bandi · November 18, 2026

A refinery can buy crude at a lower price and still have a bad week if its tanker arrives late. That was the detail I kept coming back to in Reuters’ November 16 oil report.

Two pieces of news in one price

Reuters reported that Brent and U.S. crude futures fell more than three percent. Recovering shipments through the Strait of Hormuz strengthened expectations of a surplus, even as tanker incidents put individual voyages at risk.

Those developments can happen at the same time. The market can expect more barrels over the next few months while a buyer with a shipment due Friday has good reason to worry about Friday.

Suppose a refinery has just enough inventory to reach its next delivery. A lower benchmark price helps its purchasing budget. It does little to keep the refinery running if that particular cargo misses its berth.

What I would put in the morning briefing

I’d start with two numbers on the same page: the benchmark price and the number of days the plant can operate on the crude it already has. Each answers a different buying question.

Then I’d ask the shipping team what it would cost to secure a replacement cargo that arrives in time. That quote makes the price of an extra inventory cushion easier to judge.

My reading of the selloff is that buyers gained some room to negotiate on crude. Whether they gained room to wait depends on the tanks at their own refinery.
