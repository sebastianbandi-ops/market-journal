What a solar megawatt is worth after lunch

By Sebastian Bandi

A buyer who needs electricity at dinner has a different problem from one who needs it at noon. Annual production can make two very different supply agreements look surprisingly similar.

Follow the hours

The U.S. Energy Information Administration illustrates this in California. As solar production climbs around midday, it reduces the demand other generators must serve. That remaining demand can rise quickly as the sun sets. Read EIA’s analysis of California’s duck curve.

The California example made me look again at my own generation chart for PJM. The solar line barely moves overnight, then climbs sharply through the morning. By the displayed snapshot, solar supplies 12,299 megawatts.

That chart shows solar output. To learn whether PJM experienced a comparable evening ramp, I would also need demand and output from other resources across the rest of that day.

Read the delivery terms

When I look at a power purchase agreement, I want to line up the hours a project generates with the hours its customer consumes electricity. The difference gives both parties something concrete to price.

For a buyer with a late shift, I would take a week of hourly consumption and overlay the proposed solar deliveries. Then I would highlight every hour the buyer would still need to purchase power.

That simple exercise would change the conversation. The buyer could ask what it would cost to cover those specific hours, and the developer could explain exactly which deliveries its offer includes.
