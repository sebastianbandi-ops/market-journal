Who gets to decide when a solar battery sells?

By Sebastian Bandi · November 18, 2026

Imagine a grocery chain buying solar power for its stores. At noon the panels are producing, but refrigerated cases will still need electricity at seven. A battery makes that gap a matter for negotiation.

A change in what developers are building

In October, the U.S. Energy Information Administration reported that over 16% of new utility-scale solar capacity added through September shared a site with operating battery storage. In 2017 the share was 2%.

Read the original article · U.S. Energy Information Administration, October 30, 2026

Putting a battery beside a solar plant gives the operator a choice. It can sell electricity as the panels produce it or store some for an hour when buyers will pay more.

What caught my attention was the word “operable” in EIA’s analysis. A battery in a development plan and a battery that can deliver tonight deserve different treatment in a buyer’s budget.

Put one summer evening in the contract

I’d ask the grocery chain and the developer to walk through a hot evening together. Suppose wholesale prices jump at six, an hour before the chain expects its delivery.

The developer has a reason to sell at six. The grocer has a reason to save that energy for seven. Both positions make sense, and the battery can only discharge the same stored energy once.

Before comparing bids, I’d want each seller to say who controls dispatch, how much energy it promises at seven, and who buys replacement power if the battery is empty.
